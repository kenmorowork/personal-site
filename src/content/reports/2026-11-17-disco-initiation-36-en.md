One segment, three verbs, and the widest moat in semiconductor equipment: Disco Corporation (6146, TSE Prime) is the Tokyo-based precision-processing company founded in 1937 whose dicing saws, grinders and polishers — Kiru (cut), Kezuru (grind), Migaku (polish) — sit at the back end of almost every semiconductor fab in the world, alongside the blades, wheels and other precision processing tools those machines consume. It reports as a single segment, carries no borrowings, and converts the silicon cycle into economics no peer matches: in FY3/26 (the year ended 31 March 2026, the last full 12-month year on the old March calendar) **sales were ¥436,889mn, operating profit ¥184,989mn — a 42.3% operating margin — and net income attributable to owners ¥135,521mn, with a 70.1% gross margin and ROE of 25.1%**. The initiation question is not whether Disco is a good business — ten years of history in Chapter 1, through two downturns, settle that — but what the current AI-driven peak is worth when the company's own demand gauge (shipments, 出荷額) is printing records while its guided next quarter steps down, and when the reporting calendar itself is being rebuilt: Disco is changing its fiscal year-end from March to December, so the current fiscal year (labelled FY2027 in the company's new labelling) is a transitional nine months, April–December 2026, and must never be read as a 12-month year. Strengths: (1) a near-monopoly process position — dicing and wafer-thinning grinding are bottleneck steps for HBM, advanced packaging and power semiconductors, and the installed base pulls a growing stream of high-margin consumable tools (23% of Q2 FY2027 shipments, +45% YoY); (2) balance-sheet and cash discipline through the cycle — zero interest-bearing debt in every period of the sheet, ¥184,575mn of cash at March 2026 (about ¥342.4bn including time deposits at September 2026, per the Q2 briefing), and operating cash flow that has exceeded ¥80bn for five straight years; (3) disclosure that, uniquely, separates demand from revenue — shipments lead acceptance-basis revenue, so the cycle is visible a quarter early. Weaknesses: (1) concentration — **China plus Taiwan were 57.8% of FY3/26 sales (Taiwan alone 26.9%, after a +57.8% jump)**, so export controls and cross-strait risk sit directly on the P&L; (2) the equity is priced for the plateau, not the cycle — at ¥44,000 (16 November 2026) the shares trade at 35.2x FY3/26 EPS after falling 52% from the June 2026 high, and the implied October–December operating profit in company guidance (¥58.1bn, calculated) sits about ¥8bn below the consensus the market had priced, which is what produced the October 'Disco shock'; (3) comparability breaks — the FY2023 Q3 tanshin was never published (that quarter does not exist in the archive and is not reconstructed here), the fiscal year-end change truncates the current year to nine months, and a 3-for-1 share split (April 2023) splits the per-share history. All historical figures are as reported by the company via the PL/segment sheet built from tanshin in the JPX disclosure archive; the only forward figures are company guidance and company plan figures, labelled as such, and the transitional nine-month guidance is never annualised. No rating or price target is assigned at initiation (Under Review).

## 1. Key indicators and financial history

Fiscal-year basis used throughout this report: FY3/YY means the 12 months ended 31 March 20YY (so FY3/26 = April 2025–March 2026). The current year breaks the pattern: following the change of fiscal year-end from March to December, the year labelled FY2027 in the company's new labelling is a transitional period of nine months, April–December 2026, ending 31 December 2026; it is always labelled 'transitional 9M' in this report and is never annualised. The ten-year history in this chapter and in Exhibit 1 is J-GAAP as reported in each year's own annual tanshin, compiled in the PL/segment sheet for 6146 (FY3/17–FY3/21 in the sheet's supplementary history block, FY3/22–FY3/26 in the main block, plus quarters; all template checks equal zero). Disco is a single segment by its own statement, so there is no segment history to present; the as-disclosed split is regional sales (Chapter 5, Exhibit 2). Figures are ¥mn unless stated.

The ten-year arc is a margin story laid over a cyclical top line. Sales were ¥134,204mn in FY3/17, spiked to ¥167,364mn in FY3/18 (+24.7%), fell two years to ¥141,083mn in FY3/20 as the smartphone/memory cycle turned, then compounded through the AI build-out: ¥182,857mn (FY3/21), ¥253,781mn (FY3/22, +38.8%), ¥284,135mn (FY3/23), ¥307,554mn (FY3/24), ¥393,313mn (FY3/25, +27.9%) and ¥436,889mn in FY3/26 (+11.1%) — a nine-year sales CAGR of 14.0% (calculated). Operating profit rose from ¥31,341mn to ¥184,989mn over the same window, but **the operating margin did the remarkable work: 23.4% in FY3/17, a 25.8–30.5% band through FY3/21, then 36.1% / 38.9% / 39.5% / 42.4% / 42.3% across FY3/22–26**. The driver is gross margin — 55.5% in FY3/17 to 70.1% in FY3/26 — as high-value-added grinders (wafer thinning for HBM and advanced packaging), laser and plasma dicing, and the consumable tools stream outgrew commodity dicing, while SG&A was held in a remarkably narrow band of 27.8–32.7% of sales (FY3/26: ¥121,488mn, 27.8%). Even the down years stayed highly profitable by equipment-industry standards: the FY3/20 trough still printed a 25.8% operating margin and ¥27,653mn of owners' net income, and no year in the ten made an operating or net loss.

Below operating profit the J-GAAP lines are quiet — that is part of the quality of the earnings. Non-operating items are small (FY3/26: income ¥2,143mn, expenses ¥2,196mn, mostly FX), so ordinary profit tracks operating profit closely (¥184,936mn in FY3/26). Extraordinary items are minor except FY3/24, which carried ¥7,828mn of extraordinary losses against ¥11mn of extraordinary income, cutting profit before tax to ¥114,576mn from ordinary profit of ¥122,393mn (the itemisation is in the FY3/24 tanshin; the sheet carries the totals as reported). The effective tax rate has been stable at 26–28% (FY3/26: 26.2%). Owners' net income was ¥24,203mn / ¥37,172mn / ¥28,824mn / ¥27,653mn / ¥39,091mn / ¥66,206mn / ¥82,891mn / ¥84,205mn / ¥123,891mn / ¥135,521mn across FY3/17–26, and reported ROE (tanshin basis) printed 13.9% / 19.3% / 13.6% / 12.7% / 16.4% / 24.3% / 25.9% / 22.4% / 27.6% / 25.1% — a decade averaging above 20% in the second half without financial leverage: interest-bearing debt is zero in every main-block period (the only debt in the window is ¥8,989mn of long-term borrowings at FY3/17 year-end, in the history block).

Per-share data carry one break: a 3-for-1 share split effective April 2023. Basic EPS as reported on the post-split basis was ¥611.67 in FY3/22 (restated in the FY3/23 tanshin from the originally reported ¥1,835.02), then ¥765.47 / ¥777.29 / ¥1,143.26 / ¥1,249.84 for FY3/23–26; pre-split EPS for FY3/17–21 (calculated from reported owners' net income and reported weighted shares) was ¥675.9 / ¥1,035.7 / ¥802.3 / ¥769.5 / ¥1,085.5 and is not comparable with the post-split series without dividing by three. Cash generation is the other constant: operating cash flow was positive in all ten years and exceeded ¥80bn in each of FY3/22–26 (FY3/26: ¥133,543mn, 98.5% of owners' net income), funding capex that peaked at ¥66,930mn in FY3/25 (capacity expansion) from internal resources while the dividend rose — ¥413 per share in FY3/25 and ¥505 in FY3/26 (payout 40.4%). ## 3. Business description

Disco is headquartered in Ota-ku, Tokyo, and organises its entire business around three precision-processing technologies — Kiru (cutting/dicing), Kezuru (grinding) and Migaku (polishing) — applied to semiconductor wafers and advanced electronic materials. It states in every tanshin that it is a single segment and omits segment information; the product/service note is likewise omitted as identical to the segment (sheet register S3). No product-line sales split is disclosed in the tanshin, and none is presented as disclosure in this report; the only product split available is the shipments breakdown in briefing/press materials, used in section 3b and labelled as such. The two halves of the model are equipment (dicing saws, grinders, polishers and laser/plasma processing systems) and precision processing tools — the blades, wheels and related consumables the equipment consumes — sold to the same customers from the same application-engineering base, with revenue on equipment recognised at customer acceptance inspection (a policy in place since April 2019, per the briefing notes).

## Precision processing equipment: dicing, grinding and polishing at the back end

The equipment range covers the back-end steps that turn a finished wafer into packaged devices: dicing saws that singulate wafers into chips; grinders that thin wafers (the enabling step for HBM stacks and thin advanced packages, where Disco's position is at its strongest); polishers and CMP-adjacent finishing; and laser and plasma dicing systems for applications where blade dicing reaches its limits. Demand is derived from wafer-fab and OSAT capex, weighted to leading-edge logic, memory (HBM above all in the current cycle) and power semiconductors. The financial signature of the equipment half is lumpiness by construction: units ship, then wait for customer acceptance, so quarterly revenue can move double digits on acceptance timing alone while shipments — ¥442,824mn in FY3/26, the one year for which the tanshin states a full-year shipments figure (register S5) — track underlying demand more smoothly. Equipment economics also carry the cycle: in the FY3/19–20 downturn, group sales fell 11.9% and then 4.3% and operating profit fell 24.3% and 5.7% — painful, but **the group stayed at a 25%-plus operating margin, because the consumables half (next section) does not stop when capex does**. Capacity is people- and plant-bound in Hiroshima (Kuwabata Works) and Nagano (Chino Works), with the Gohara Plant at Hiroshima Works (decision announced 18 April 2025) and the Haneda R&D Center new building the current expansion projects; capex was ¥35,227mn in FY3/26 after a ¥66,930mn peak in FY3/25. Unit volumes, capacity and utilisation are not disclosed in the tanshin and are not quantified here. Source: PL/segment sheet (tanshin, JPX archive); Q2 FY2027 briefing materials, 22 October 2026.

## Precision processing tools (consumables): the recurring layer

Blades, grinding wheels and related tools are consumed in proportion to wafers processed, not capex approved, which makes this the group's shock absorber and its margin engine: consumables carry the installed base's economics, are specified into customer processes alongside the machines, and are hard to substitute without requalifying the process. The disclosed colour is shipments-based: tools were 23% of Q2 FY2027 shipments and grew 45% YoY (+24% QoQ) — faster than equipment — per the company shipment breakdown carried in the interview pack (press analysis of company disclosure). No tools sales or profit figure is disclosed in the tanshin for any period (register S3/S8), so this report does not split the P&L; the profit share of tools is an explicit interview question, because the 'recurring revenue dampens the downturn' claim now made for Disco cannot be verified from disclosure until it is answered. What can be verified is the consolidated outcome the mix produces: **a gross margin that has risen through the cycle (55.5% in FY3/17 to 70.1% in FY3/26; 71.7% in Q2 FY2027) and a down-cycle operating-margin floor of about 26% in the FY3/19–20 downturn**. Source: interview pack, 16 November 2026; PL/segment sheet.

## Applications, services and the demand map

By application, the current cycle is led by generative-AI data-centre investment — HBM thinning and grinding, advanced-packaging dicing and polishing — which the company names as the driver of H1 FY2027 equipment shipments, with power semiconductors (including SiC processing, where grinding and laser processes are the contested steps) and conventional logic/memory capacity behind it. The regional footprint of that demand is visible in Chapter 5: Taiwan (TSMC and its packaging chain) jumped to 26.9% of FY3/26 sales, and China — a mix of advanced and mature-node capacity that the company does not split — is the largest single market at 30.9%. Services, application support and process development are integral to the model (acceptance support is part of the sale, and the application-engineering relationship is the moat's maintenance mechanism) but are not separately disclosed. Employees, order intake and backlog are not disclosed in the tanshin — Disco reports shipments instead of orders — and are shown as not disclosed (未取得) in this report. Source: Q2 FY2027 briefing materials; PL/segment sheet register S8.

## The technology frame: process monopoly, acceptance accounting and the WILL culture

Disco's competitive claim is process depth rather than product breadth: decades of accumulated know-how in cutting, grinding and polishing brittle advanced materials, an applications library built machine by machine at customer sites, and vertical integration into the consumables those processes consume. The substitutes are partial — rival dicing/grinding suppliers exist at the commodity end, and process shifts (panel-level packaging, hybrid bonding preparation, plasma dicing) redraw individual steps — but no competitor replicates the full Kiru–Kezuru–Migaku chain with the same installed base, which is why through-cycle pricing holds: the FY3/24 accounts show a 67.8% gross margin in a year when sales grew only 8.2%. Two structural features follow for the equity. First, acceptance-basis revenue recognition (since April 2019) makes reported revenue a lagging, lumpy series — shipments are the leading indicator and are disclosed quarterly, a transparency gift no peer in this programme matches. Second, management runs the company on through-cycle targets rather than annual budgets (Chapter 7) and a distinctive internal culture (the 'WILL' system of employee-chosen work allocation, widely documented in company materials) aimed at the engineer retention on which the applications moat depends; this report notes the system as company-described culture, not as a verified performance driver. Source: Q2 FY2027 briefing materials (acceptance-basis note); company materials via the interview pack.

## 4. Market and value chain

Disco sits at the back end of the semiconductor value chain: it sells capital equipment and consumables to wafer fabs (IDMs and foundries), memory makers and OSATs, downstream of front-end WFE (lithography, etch, deposition) and upstream of packaging and test. Its demand is therefore a derivative of leading-edge capacity additions — today concentrated in AI accelerators and HBM — filtered through two Disco-specific gears: ==acceptance timing, which shifts revenue between quarters, and the consumables stream, which follows wafer starts rather than capex==. The verifiable demand evidence in the sources used here is the company's own shipments series (¥442,824mn in FY3/26 as disclosed in that year's tanshin; ¥284.5bn in H1 FY2027, +37.2% YoY) and its regional sales (Chapter 5); no company-disclosed market-size figure for dicing/grinding is carried in the tanshin, and third-party share estimates (Shared Research's teardown of SCREEN's report cites Disco's R&D efficiency — five-year cumulative operating profit divided by prior-five-year R&D — at 855.7%, the highest of the four Japanese SPE majors compared there) are context, not disclosure.

Competition is asymmetric. In dicing, Disco faces domestic and overseas saw makers at standard specifications; in wafer thinning/grinding for advanced applications, and in the integrated equipment-plus-tools offer, it has no equivalent-scale rival in the sources used here — the moat is the process library, the applications engineers embedded at customers, and the consumables pull-through, and the entry barrier is qualification: a back-end process step written into an HBM production flow is not re-tendered on price. The realistic threats are therefore technological and geopolitical rather than commercial: a process shift that routes around grinding/dicing steps; Chinese domestic equipment makers taking the mature-node China demand (30.9% of FY3/26 sales) under export-control pressure; and customer concentration in the AI capex complex (Taiwan 26.9% of sales). The equity sensitivities follow directly: the yen (a ¥10bn-plus gross-margin swing factor between the Q2 realised rates and the Q3 assumed rates, per the guidance bridge), China/Taiwan demand durability, and the tools mix that decides how much of the 42–47% operating margin survives the next capex pause. Sources: PL/segment sheet (tanshin, JPX archive); Q2 FY2027 briefing materials; Shared Research teardown notes (SCREEN report), programme files.

## 7. Strategy and mid-term plan — DISCO VISION 2030 and the through-cycle targets

Disco's long-term frame, DISCO VISION 2030, deliberately sets no sales or profit amount targets — a policy choice the company justifies by the silicon cycle: single-year amount targets would mismeasure a business whose quarterly operating margin has ranged from the mid-20s to 47% within this decade. Its only quantitative management targets are through-cycle floors: a four-year cumulative ordinary-profit margin of at least 20% and a four-year cumulative RORA (return on risk assets) of at least 20%. **Both are currently exceeded by roughly double — on the latest four-year window to FY3/26 the figures were 41.4% and 51.2% respectively** (securities-report compilation via the interview pack) — which makes the targets a downside covenant rather than a plan: they bind in the downturn, not at the peak. Dividend policy is results-linked: the interim dividend for the transitional year was set at ¥186 per share, calculated at a 25% payout on H1 results, and the FY3/26 annual dividend of ¥505 represented a 40.4% payout; the year-end dividend for the transitional year is undecided and no forecast is published (company policy). Guidance practice is part of the strategy's conservatism: one quarter ahead only, cumulatively stated, with FX assumptions disclosed (USD/JPY 151.0 / EUR/JPY 173.0 for Oct–Dec 2026) — the market's recurring pattern of pricing more than the guide (the October 2026 'Disco shock' being the latest instance) is the cost of that policy, and management has not changed it.

Capacity and investment plans, as stated at the Q2 FY2027 briefing: capex of about ¥33.0bn, depreciation of about ¥16.0bn and R&D of about ¥38.0bn for the year as labelled in the materials (unchanged from April), with expenditure continuing on the Haneda R&D Center new building, the new plant at Hiroshima Works (Gohara Plant, decided April 2025) and rationalisation investment — the company states annual capex above ¥30bn is expected to continue. The monitoring set for the transitional year's final quarter (Q3 FY2027 print, expected late January 2027), in the order the data arrives: (1) October–December shipments against the ~¥149.7bn implied by the nine-month outlook of ¥434,200mn — the cleanest demand read, before acceptance and FX noise; (2) October–December operating profit against the ~¥58.1bn implied outlook (calculated) — settles the sandbagging-versus-plateau debate; (3) the shipped-but-unaccepted gap (H1: ¥27.5bn, calculated) and the January–March 2027 one-quarter outlook that will accompany the print, plus the tools share of shipments (Q2: 23%). All plan figures above are company targets, company plans or company guidance, labelled as such; this report contains no J-Core Fundamental Research forecasts.
